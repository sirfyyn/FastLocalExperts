Abstract

We run Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 512-expert Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model, on a single workstation with one 32 GB GPU, 120 GB of system RAM and 16 CPU cores. Two pieces make this practical. First, FLE-IQ3_M (internally G3), a hybrid quantization that combines the Unsloth UD-IQ4_XS release with expert layers from ISTA-DASLab's GSQ-RCO release: 88.87 GB instead of 93.68 GB, with a KL divergence against BF16 that stays within the measurement noise on three texts, one of them held out. Second, FLE, a fork of llama.cpp that borrows ideas and some code from ik_llama.cpp, FreeToken, KTransformers and Strata (Section 8): a GPU expert cache with frequency-gated admission and oldest-first eviction, overlap of GPU cache hits with CPU work on cache misses, fused CUDA kernels, and speculative decoding with the model's own multi-token-prediction (MTP) head, its experts requantized to IQ4_NL. Against current upstream llama.cpp (commit a8c9a4e7c, newer than FLE's base), with the same FLE-IQ3_M file, the same card and the same 16 threads (no expert cache, no MTP), the final FLE configuration decodes 2.49 times as fast as upstream with micro-batch 1024 (−59.9 % [95 % CI −61.2 .. −58.5] for upstream in a paired bracket, B1e) and 2.78 times as fast as upstream with micro-batch 4096 (−64.0 % [−65.2 .. −62.9]); FLE runs at about 100 tokens/s on these prompts. Prefill is where FLE still loses: upstream with micro-batch 4096 processes 4k and 8.6k prompts 24 % and 12 % faster, and Strata, the closest public engine, 2.3 to 2.7 times as fast, while FLE decodes 23 % faster than Strata. Against our own previous production setup (stock build with Unsloth UD-IQ4_XS, ~56 tokens/s) FLE was +58.5 % faster [+55.8 .. +61.6] already before the last steps (bracket END2). On three benchmarks from Qwen's model card, one run each, FLE-IQ3_M on FLE is level with the card on IFBench (80.3 strict / 83.0 loose against 81.3) and GPQA Diamond (88.9 against 91.7, inside the 95 % interval) and below it on LiveCodeBench v6 (85.5 against 91.9, outside the interval; 10 of the 19 failures are answers cut off at the 81,920-token limit). Besides FLE-IQ3_M we release two smaller hybrids and FLE-IQ3_M-REAP448, with 64 of 512 experts per layer pruned by REAP; a 400-question MKQA knowledge probe shows that KLD on a few texts understates what pruning removes (at 384 experts per layer the KLD rose moderately, while correct answers fell by 5.8 / 11.8 points in German / English). We report the negative results as well: expert prefetching by pre-gating, n-gram drafting, low-rank kernels and fully self-made quantizations all lost in our measurements, and so did copying part of the cache misses over PCIe as Strata does (−4.5 %) and a rebase of the fork onto current upstream (−5.7 %). A re-trained MTP head (recursive self-distillation) gained +4.54 % only on the tasks its training corpus contained; on 16 unseen tasks the untrained head was +2.95 % [+1.96 .. +3.93] faster than it, so the release ships the untrained head. The speed numbers above were measured with the re-trained head c2 (Section 4.3).

1 Introduction

Large MoE models activate only a few experts per token, so most of their weights are idle at any moment. On a workstation this invites a split: keep what every token needs on the GPU and leave the rarely used experts in system RAM. The split is easy to state and hard to make fast. A token that needs an expert which is not on the GPU waits for the CPU (or for a PCIe transfer), and with ten active experts out of 512 per layer, such misses happen in almost every layer of almost every token.

This report describes how we made that split fast for one concrete model on one concrete machine, and how we checked every step. It is not a general system. It is a measured account of what worked, what did not, and by how much, written so that someone with similar hardware can reproduce it.

Contributions:

  1. FLE-IQ3_M, a hybrid quantization of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next that is 4.81 GB smaller than Unsloth UD-IQ4_XS at the same measured quality (Section 3).
  2. An expert cache inside llama.cpp with admission and eviction policies, and overlap of GPU hits with CPU misses (Sections 4.1–4.2).
  3. The model's MTP head in llama.cpp, its experts requantized to IQ4_NL, with a reduced draft vocabulary, and a negative result: re-training the head by recursive self-distillation on the model's own outputs did not generalize to unseen tasks (Section 4.3).
  4. A measurement method for speed on a noisy single machine: paired brackets (reference before, candidates, reference after) with drift limits and bootstrap intervals (Section 2.3).
  5. Negative results with the same rigor, including a pre-gating prefetcher that a simulation predicted to be the largest lever and that measured −10 % to −27 % (Section 6).

2 Setup and method

2.1 Hardware

One workstation ("Titan"): AMD Ryzen 9 9950X3D (16 cores, 32 threads), 120 GB DDR5 RAM, NVIDIA RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell (32 GB) as the only GPU used for inference; a second GPU (RTX PRO 2000, 16 GB) took the early KLD measurements. Linux kernel 7.0, NVIDIA driver 610.43. The CPU experts run on cores 0–15 (one thread per physical core). In the final configuration the expert weights live in a pinned host buffer (--load-mode none) instead of a memory-mapped file in the page cache (Section 4.6).

2.2 Model

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next [Qwen Team, arXiv:2608.30320] is a sparse MoE model with 125 B parameters, 6 B of them active per token, plus 51 B parameters of n-gram embedding tables that Qwen keeps off the accelerator. Its 48 layers repeat three Gated DeltaNet (GDN) layers followed by one Qwen Sparse Attention (QSA) layer, and every layer has a MoE block with 512 routed experts, 10 active per token, and one shared expert. The residual stream is widened to four branches and read and written through Qwen's Gated Residual, a member of the hyper-connection family [Zhu et al., arXiv:2409.19606]; the configuration and our kernels call it hyper-connections (hc_count 4). A one-layer MTP module ships with the weights. The n-gram embedding (Qwen's term; ple_* keys in the Hugging Face configuration and tensor per_layer_token_embd in the GGUF, hence "PLE table" below) is a single hashed bigram/trigram lookup at the second decoder layer, stored as 16 sub-tables of about 20 M rows with 160 values each, 51.2 B weights in total.

2.3 Measuring quality

Quality is the mean KL divergence (KLD) of the quantized model's next-token distribution against BF16, plus top-1 agreement, following the argument that equal accuracy can hide many flipped answers, so that a distance metric such as KL divergence should be reported as well [Dutta et al., arXiv:2407.09141].

Development texts (F, E, V). FLE-IQ3_M was selected on three internal texts: F (used for selecting layers), E (check), V (held out until the end). Acceptance criterion, fixed in advance: mean KLD on F and E at most two combined standard errors above the reference (≈ +0.0078), top-1 at most 0.5 percentage points worse; V must confirm. F and V contain internal material and are not published; they remain an internal cross-check.

Public texts (E, W, K) for the comparison. All numbers in Section 7 use three texts anyone can rebuild with scripts/fle/bench/b6_texte.py (sources and SHA-256 in quellen.json): E, the Python language reference (pydoc topics, PSF license, 63 KB); W, four German Wikipedia articles at fixed revisions (quantization in signal processing, floating-point number, main memory, graphics processor; CC BY-SA 4.0, 81 KB); K, seven files of the German Kubernetes documentation (content/de/docs/concepts at commit 4472af456106; CC BY 4.0, 47 KB).

Neutral meter. KLD for the comparison is computed with upstream llama-perplexity at commit a8c9a4e7c without any FLE patch, so that no quantization is measured with our own kernels: -c 2048 -b 2048 -ub 2048 -t 16 -ngl 99 -cmoe -fit off with LLAMA_ATTN_ROT_DISABLE=1, GPU0, cores 0–15, inference server stopped. The BF16 reference (354 GB GGUF, converted from the release) is computed with the same build and settings; one pass over 2048 tokens takes 95 s. As a check of the meter, FLE-IQ3_M on the internal texts gives F 0.1505 / E 0.0516 / V 0.0929 with the neutral build against 0.1475 / 0.0511 / 0.0905 with our own build. This check ran earlier, on GPU1 with 8 threads, and reused the BF16 reference logits computed with our own build, so it tests the quantized side only.

Noise floor. The same file, text and build reproduce bit-exactly, but this model amplifies any change in computation order because expert selection flips: two runs of FLE-IQ3_M on GPU0 and GPU1 (16 and 8 threads, builds a few days apart) give 0.0905 and 0.0975 on text V; switching all CUDA fusions off (GGML_CUDA_DISABLE_FUSION=1) changes V by −0.0025; and the logits at micro-batch 2048 measured against those at micro-batch 4096 (context 4096) differ by a KLD of 0.062 (top-1 agreement 92.7 %). We therefore compare KLD only on the same card, build and settings, and treat differences below ~0.01 on a single text as no finding.

Knowledge probe (MKQA). KLD on three short texts can miss knowledge that a change removes, which matters for expert pruning. The pruned files are therefore also asked 400 factual questions from MKQA [Longpre et al., arXiv:2007.15207] (open-domain export nthakur/mkqa-open-domain, answerable questions only), the same questions in German and in English. The system prompt asks for the answer only, or exactly "I don't know"; sampling as on Qwen's card (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, min-p 0), thinking off, up to 256 tokens, one seed per question shared by all files (paired). An answer is correct when one of MKQA's German or English answer aliases appears in it after lower-casing and removing accents and punctuation; abstentions count as not correct. Differences are paired bootstrap intervals (2,000 draws). Server: the production FLE build (patch 51), experts on the CPU, no MTP.

2.4 Measuring speed: paired brackets

Decode speed on this machine drifts by several percent over a day. Every speed claim in this report comes from a bracket: reference run, candidate runs, reference run again, all on the same prompts and server settings; the bracket is valid only if the two reference runs differ by at most 5 %. Candidates are compared with the reference per request (paired) and summarized with a bootstrap 95 % interval (m2_gepaart.py). Numbers from different brackets are never compared directly. The standard prompt set has 24 requests (code 9, math 6, physics 3, prose 6). Where a result comes from alternating arms (B A B A) without this pairing, it is marked as such.

3 Quantization: the FLE-IQ3_M hybrid

Start from Unsloth UD-IQ4_XS (93.68 GB file, 64.87 GB resident without the PLE table). Replace the expert tensors of 34 of the 48 MoE layers with the corresponding tensors from ISTA-DASLab's GSQ-RCO GGUFs (IQ3_S / IQ3_XXS) [GSQ arXiv:2604.18556, RCO arXiv:2605.00649]. Result: 88.87 GB file, 60.06 GB resident. KLD against BF16, relative to UD-IQ4_XS, as used for the acceptance decision: F −0.0079, E +0.0031, V +0.0005, top-1 +0.11 / +0.08 / +0.81 pp, so FLE-IQ3_M passes. These values were measured in decode mode, the way the files run in production (our build, GPU0, micro-batch 4; FLE-IQ3_M with 173 cache slots, the cache bias and the cache policy of Section 4.1, UD-IQ4_XS with 160 slots and no bias). The files alone pass as well: −0.0077 / +0.0029 / −0.0026 with our build at micro-batch 2048, and −0.0035 / +0.0038 / +0.0011 with the neutral meter (Section 2.3).

What did not pass: our own full quantizations (KLD on F 0.259–0.269 against 0.155 for Unsloth), Q5_0 dense weights (F +0.024 against Unsloth, z = +5.8, top-1 −1.63 pp), fewer active experts (k = 8: +0.054 and +0.084 on F, depending on how the router weights are renormalized), a cache bias of 0.002 together with the cache policy (top-1 on F −0.54 pp), and the larger hybrids G4 (35 donor layers, 0.8 GB smaller than FLE-IQ3_M; top-1 on F −0.51 pp, just over the limit) and G6 (40 donor layers, 2.2 GB smaller; KLD on F and E more than two standard errors above the reference, top-1 on F −0.93 pp).

Positioning: FLE-IQ3_M is smaller than UD-IQ4_XS, not smaller than pure ISTA quants; the claim is "IQ4_XS quality at less size", and ISTA is the donor of most expert layers.

Layer selection. For every candidate donor and every MoE layer we predict the damage of swapping that layer's expert tensors as the relative output error err = |y_Q − y_BF16|² / |y_BF16|², measured on captured layer inputs (quant_sensitivity.py, 4096 tokens). The file is assembled from whole expert layers (gate, up and down together) with gguf_splice.py; everything else comes from the base file.

Smaller stages (FLE-IQ3_XXS, FLE-IQ2_M). To give a curve of size against KLD against speed instead of a single point, we build more hybrids with the same tools (two of three planned classes are buildable, see below). The selection is a knapsack: for a fixed target size it picks per layer the donor with the smallest predicted error (dynamic programming over 16 MB steps, tools/fle-hybrid/fle_select.py in the engine repository, with gguf_splice.py and gguf_provenance.py). FLE-IQ3_M itself was chosen the other way round, as the largest saving under an error bound. Recomputing it with the knapsack at its size of 88.87 GB keeps 44 of the 48 layers on the same source: one base layer moves to ISTA IQ3_S and three ISTA IQ3_S layers move to ISTA IQ3_XXS, which gives 35 instead of 34 donor layers and 88.51 GB.

4 Engine

4.1 Expert cache on the GPU

Every MoE layer whose experts stay in host memory (46 layers for FLE-IQ3_M; the memory fit decides the number) gets a cache of expert slots on the GPU, 173 per layer before the byte budget below. Admission is ranked: the misses of a step are sorted by an aged request count (counters halved every 2,000 observations per layer, FLE_MOE_ADMIT_FREQ=2000), and at most one expert per layer and step enters the cache. Eviction takes the least frequent of the 32 oldest entries (FLE_MOE_EVICT_OLDEST=32). A small router bias towards cached experts (0.001 in probability units) changes which experts are chosen, not their weights, and keeps the outputs within the KLD criterion (Section 3). Bracket HZ2 (reference Unsloth UD-IQ4_XS; all arms with 160 slots per layer and n-max 1; reference drift +0.38 %): FLE-IQ3_M alone +4.05 % [+2.92 .. +5.19], FLE-IQ3_M with bias and policy +12.15 % [+10.68 .. +13.57].

The smaller FLE-IQ3_M file frees GPU memory, but only turning that memory into cache slots makes it faster. Bracket V6b (Section 4.4) measures the slope: 146 instead of 173 slots cost −4.39 %, 120 slots −10.04 %. An earlier bracket that compared slot counts against UD-IQ4_XS (HY) is not cited: its three candidate arms ran under memory pressure (cgroup memory-high events in all three, none in the reference arms), with half the output limit and 19 instead of 24 paired requests (Appendix A).

The final build replaces the fixed slot count by a global byte budget (patch 44): the cache gets a number of bytes, the slots are distributed over all layers with host experts by a usage profile (118 to 248 slots per layer for FLE-IQ3_M in the final brackets B3e5 and B3e6, mean 174), and the profile is saved every 600 s and loaded at the next start (warm start). The effect is measured in Section 5.3 (bracket CG): +1.49 % [+0.64 .. +2.41] with the profile, and a much smaller loss when memory gets tight.

4.3 Speculative decoding with the MTP head, and a re-training that did not generalize

Draft-and-verify [Leviathan et al., arXiv:2211.17192; Chen et al., arXiv:2302.01318] with the model's own MTP module [DeepSeek-AI, arXiv:2412.19437; Gloeckle et al., arXiv:2404.19737]. With the shipped head, the conditional acceptance derived from the tokens per cycle (Section 4.4) is about 0.86 / 0.78 / 0.78 for the first, second and third drafted token, and two drafted tokens (n-max 2) give the shortest time per token in that measurement. The bracket meant to confirm the choice directly (NM2) drifted by −5.88 % and is invalid (Appendix A).

4.4 Where the time goes

Cycle model T(n) = T0 + c·M(n+1) + Σ DG_j (M = cache misses, DG = extra cost of the j-th drafted token), calibrated on single requests of the base build of 2026-09-30 (bracket NM: untrained head, fused Gated Residual and overlap not yet switched on): T0 = 22.6 ms, about 82 % of a single-token cycle of 27.4 ms. Measured cycles for n-max 1/2/3: 27.4 / 34.6 / 42.8 ms for 1.86 / 2.53 / 3.06 tokens (n-max 3: 18 valid requests). Three parameters on three points describe these measurements but do not test the model. An Nsight Systems profile of the production build of 2026-10-04 (before the final configuration; 15 s window, n-max 2, 77.3 tokens/s) shows the GPU busy 64.5 % of the time; the gaps split into launch 17.3 %, CPU after the GPU 7.6 %, CPU before 6.4 %, wake-up 2.0 % and GPU-internal 0.9 %. A gap between consecutive GPU operations averages 27 µs; a full GPU–CPU–GPU round trip averages 226 µs (median 55 µs).

Sensitivity to cache misses, measured directly (bracket V6b, 2026-10-06; same build and 46 cache layers, only the slot count changes; reference drift +0.68 %): 173 slots give 30.3 misses per step (hit rate 91.2 %), 146 slots 38.5 (+27 %) and 120 slots 52.3 (+72 %); speed −4.39 % [−5.43 .. −3.37] and −10.04 % [−10.74 .. −9.37]. Each percent more misses costs 0.14–0.16 % speed, less than the cycle model predicts; our reading is that the overlap, switched on after the model was calibrated, hides part of the CPU time. The slope is measured only towards more misses.

4.5 Prefill and prompt cache

Lean prefill at micro-batch 1024 (patch 35), bracket P35Q against micro-batch 512 (drift −0.30 %): micro-batch 1024 alone costs decode speed, −19.01 % [−19.76 .. −18.26]; our reading (not measured separately) is that the larger compute buffer takes GPU memory from the expert cache. With the memory fit lowered from 19156 to 18806 MiB, decode is +2.26 % [+1.24 .. +3.29]. The same effect shows in END2: the 1024 candidate without fit lost −20.36 %. Prefill of an 8.6k prompt: about 425 instead of about 280 tokens/s (two cold runs per arm, 2026-10-03; the final configuration reaches about 1,250 tokens/s, Section 4.6). Prompt-cache protection for the interactive slot (patches 39/39b): a follow-up turn at 7.1k context took 2.4 s (516 tokens recomputed, the rest restored from the RAM cache); computing the same context from scratch took 17.5 s (single probe, 2026-10-03).

4.6 Final configuration: threads, pinned experts, slot release, byte budget, ubatch lending

Three steps after END2 lead to the intermediate configuration p43-pin; four more, taken over from Strata or found while measuring against it (Section 5.3), lead to the final configuration p49-ring that all final numbers use:

  • 16 threads on cores 0–15 instead of 8 on cores 0–7: +5.23 % [+3.64 .. +6.86] (bracket RB2, paired). The CPU computes the cache misses (≈ 6 % of expert calls at 94 % hits), so more cores shorten the critical path.
  • Slot release (patch 43): when a conversation switches slots, the time to first token falls by 202.5 ms (−25.6 %); outputs stay within GPU noise (alternating arms, not a paired bracket).
  • Pinned experts (--load-mode none, no patch): expert weights are read once into a pinned host buffer, so every host-to-device copy runs at pinned-memory speed. Against a warm production run (model fully in the page cache before every reference arm; alternating arms B A B A, medians, not a paired bracket): prefill of 1000 words 446 → 852 tokens/s (+91 %), 3000 words 558 → 1037 tokens/s (+86 %); time to first token −309 ms for prompts of about 50 tokens and −613 ms for about 490; decode 77.4 → 84.5 tokens/s (+9.1 %). Cost: 54 GB of RAM are pinned and can no longer be reclaimed by the page cache (available memory after loading 47.5 instead of 100.5 GiB); loading takes 55 instead of 45 s. Greedy outputs of the longest task differed between all arms, also between arms of the same build (GPU noise). The decode gain is not decomposed and was not repeated in a paired bracket; our candidate explanation is that cache-miss copies also run from pinned memory.
  • Byte budget and warm start for the expert cache (patch 44): one budget of 17,035 MiB for all layers instead of a fixed slot count, planned from a saved usage profile; +1.49 % [+0.64 .. +2.41] (bracket CG, Section 5.3).
  • AVX2 dot product for Q2_0 (patch 45): matters only for files with Q2_0 tensors (Section 6, Section 7).
  • Ubatch lending (patch 48): decode runs with a 1,024-token micro-batch and the full cache; a prefill call of 2,048 tokens or more lends whole expert-cache layers from the back for the larger compute buffer and gives them back afterwards (bracket W7: 15 layers and 5,033 MiB at micro-batch 4096, 2,327 slots refilled from pinned RAM in 106 ms; in the final configuration, with the prefetch ring below, 19 layers, 6,409 MiB and 2,952 slots in 136 ms). Bracket W7 (drift −0.26 %): prefill 1k / 4k / 8.6k ±0 / +30.0 % / +53.7 %; decode −0.25 % [−1.15 .. +0.66].
  • Prefetch ring (patch 49): while lending, a second CUDA stream copies the whole weights of the next expert splits into a ring of three staging buffers (450 MiB each, also lent from the cache), so the copy overlaps the computation and the per-layer synchronization on the routed ids disappears. Greedy answers of about 400 characters are identical to the build without it for prompts of 1k, 4k and 8.6k tokens. Bracket W49c (drift +0.35 %): prefill 4k +10.6 %, 8.6k +14.2 % on top of patch 48; decode −0.18 % [−0.91 .. +0.49]. An nsys timeline shows why not more: each layer has three weight splits, up and gate compute shorter than their copy takes at 57.5 GB/s, so about 0.64 s of copy without computation remain per 8.6k prompt; six buffers instead of three did not help (1,220 against 1,249 tokens/s at 8.6k).

After the final measurement one correctness fix went into production (patch 50, a server change): a request that names a slot which is still busy was allowed to load its cached prompt into that slot before it was deferred, which replaced the running generation's state (upstream behaviour of get_available_slot; it corrupted our first benchmark runs, Section 5.4). The fix does not touch the computation; speed was not re-measured with it.

A first run was invalid, and why. The first IFBench and GPQA runs (2026-10-06/07) sent their requests directly to the server while the gateway in front of it assigned interactive requests to slots by id_slot. In upstream get_available_slot, a request that names a slot which is still processing gets that slot returned; because no cached prompt matches, the function then runs the prompt-cache update on the busy slot (prompt_save and prompt_load), which replaces the KV and recurrent state of the running generation with the waiting request's cached prompt, on every retry until the slot is free. The running answer then continued inside another conversation or stopped early. Long generations were hit hardest, because their own state exceeds the prompt-cache limit, is not saved, and therefore never evicts the waiting entry. A canary test (scripts/fle/bench/slot_kanarien.py: a long answer on slot 0, then a request for slot 0 with a different cached conversation) flipped 3 of 3 rounds into the other conversation; with patch 50 (return the busy slot untouched, the caller defers the task) 0 of 3, the waiting request was deferred for 15–17 s and the long answer ran to its full length. Both invalid runs were discarded and all three benchmarks were re-run on the fixed server.

6 Negative results

Pre-gating prefetch (router top-K of layer l+1 on the input of layer l, asynchronous loads): a trace simulation gave +18 .. +25 % for a single stream (+24 .. +35 % with two parallel slots), assuming free room for the prefetch slots and leaving out the cost of the extra copies. Offline, the router inputs predicted 57–81 % of the misses one layer ahead (prefetch width 10 to 24), within the simulated range. Measured: −9.67 % [−11.14 .. −8.22] (bracket VL, reference drift −1.28 %), worse with more prefetch slots (−12.49 %, −27.34 %); the extra copies cost more than the hits save. Registering the memory-mapped model file in place with cudaHostRegister failed (0 of 3 ranges); the final configuration instead reads the experts into a separately allocated pinned buffer (Section 4.6).

Smaller items. N-gram drafting before MTP: −13.77 % [−18.86 .. −8.58] and −23.34 % [−30.02 .. −16.12] (bracket NG; acceptance falls from 78 % to 66–71 %). Low-rank kernels for the Gated Residual (patches 30/30b) were slower than the existing kernel chain in micro-benchmarks (49 against 28 µs per mix) and are not enabled. GRAPH_OPT=1 changed the step cost by −1.7 to −0.4 %, inside the spread of the reference runs (up to 7 %). Q6_K dense weights pass quality but give no speed.

Re-training the MTP head. Details in Section 4.3. The release ships the untrained head; measurement tasks and training corpus must be disjoint.

Rebase onto current upstream. We rebased the fork's changes onto a newer upstream commit. A first bracket (RB2) gave −47.21 %, but the rebased build fitted only 39 instead of 46 cache layers on the GPU. Under equal conditions (bracket RB3b: fit target 18300, 165 slots, 47 cache layers against 46, both 16 threads; reference drift +2.4 %) the rebased build is still −5.74 % [−6.87 .. −4.61], across all prompt types (code −5.0, math −6.0, physics −9.3, prose −4.7 %), although the rebased build accepted slightly more drafted tokens (82.1 against 81.1 %). The same bracket RB2 shows that the slot count alone does not explain it: our build with 165 instead of 173 slots −0.23 % [−1.38 .. +1.08]. We stay on the older base. Whether the loss comes from upstream changes or from differences in the rebased stack is not decomposed.

Copying part of the misses over PCIe (patch 47). Strata copies about half of the missed experts to the GPU instead of computing them on the CPU. Our version (a three-slot ring per layer, filled ahead of the GPU chain) served up to 40.6 % of the non-cached experts and lost −4.5 % decode speed (Section 5.3), because the copies delay the hit chain on the same stream. A separate copy stream with one layer of lead would be the next variant; it is not built.

Strata's window graph. Enqueuing a whole verification window on the GPU and signalling layers from the host (Strata's design) was not built: Strata's fixed time per window, 11.9 ms (95 % 8.7–15.4 ms), is not clearly below ours (about 15.2 ms), and the largest fixed item in it is itself a wait for the GPU (Section 5.3). The hand-off mechanism itself is cheap (1.8 µs), so the idea stays available if a later measurement shows a gap.

Q2_0 without a vector kernel. Before patch 45 the Q2_0 dot product had only a scalar implementation on x86, which made every file with Q2_0 experts slow on the CPU side: ISTA Q2_0 −25.72 %, FLE-IQ2_M −5.11 % against FLE-IQ3_M at 173 slots; with the AVX2 kernel +3.84 % and +5.49 % at 173 slots (earlier engine state), and +8.49 % and +11.60 % in the final measurement at equal budget (Section 7).

8 Related work

Offloading MoE experts between GPU and CPU: Fiddler [Kamahori et al., arXiv:2402.07033] orchestrates CPU and GPU so that experts in host memory are computed on the CPU instead of moving their weights; Mixtral offloading [Eliseev & Mazur, arXiv:2312.17238] combines an LRU expert cache with speculative loading and mixed quantization; MoE-Infinity [Xue et al., arXiv:2401.14361] guides expert caching and prefetching by traced activation patterns; HybriMoE [Zhong et al., arXiv:2504.05897] balances expert computation between CPU and GPU with impact-driven prefetching and score-based caching. HOBBIT [Tang et al., arXiv:2411.01433] replaces less critical cache-miss experts with low-precision copies and adds layer-level prefetching and a multidimensional expert cache on top of llama.cpp. MoE-SpAc [Li et al., arXiv:2603.09983] uses speculative decoding as a look-ahead for expert utility that drives prefetching, eviction and CPU/GPU balancing, and SpecMoEOff [Wang et al., arXiv:2508.21706] uses it to raise the work per offloaded expert. Like HOBBIT, FLE is built on llama.cpp; it differs in running IQ-quantized GGUF files of one model on a single 32 GB card, in drafting with the model's own MTP head, and in measuring every step in paired brackets, including an attempt to re-train that head that did not generalize (Section 4.3).

FLE builds directly on four projects. ik_llama.cpp (Kawrakow, MIT): the trellis quant types IQ2/3/4_KT and their CUDA dequantization are taken over essentially verbatim (NOTICE-ik_llama.md in the engine repository). FreeToken [Yang et al., arXiv:2608.16157; code by FlashML-org, Apache 2.0]: the bandwidth-matched split of missed experts between PCIe transfers and CPU execution, the default rule of its profiler that chooses hybrid execution when the CPU bandwidth exceeds twice the PCIe bandwidth, and the hybrid expert path are re-implemented in C++, with a provenance note in each file. KTransformers (Apache 2.0): the idea of computing cold experts with optimized CPU operators next to a GPU-resident hot set; an experimental port of its CPU operators was never part of the build and is not shipped. Strata (Niko1221, MIT): the slot-release change (Section 4.6) and further scheduling ideas, with file and line references in each commit. The pairing of two quant blocks per 512-bit vector in llama.cpp PR #27590 ("ggml-cpu: add AVX-512 and VNNI paths for Q5_K/Q6_K dot products", still open) informed our AVX-512 IQ4_NL dot product on the CPU side of the expert path. Quantization: importance matrix and IQ quants in llama.cpp [Kawrakow, llama.cpp #4861 (importance matrix), #4897 (IQ2 quantization with an importance matrix), #5590 (IQ4_NL), #5676 (IQ3_S, IQ3_M)]; GSQ and RCO as above.

9 Limitations and reproducibility

One model, one machine; effects of policies tuned on this hardware may not transfer. The task benchmarks (Section 5.4) are single runs with a 81,920-token output limit, without a run of the unquantized model, so they do not separate quantization loss from engine, harness and chance; LiveCodeBench is below Qwen's card. The MKQA probe (Section 3) checks short factual answers only, with one sampled answer per question, and was run on the pruned files, FLE-IQ3_M and FLE-IQ3_XXS, not on FLE-IQ2_M or the public files. Builds, patches, scripts and raw data are listed in Appendix A; the gaps found in the inventory are listed there too.

Availability. The engine is published as FastLocalExperts at https://github.com/sirfyyn/FastLocalExperts under the MIT license of llama.cpp; tag v1.0 is the configuration measured here (final profile p49-ring) plus the slot fix of patch 50 and the crash report of patch 51, neither of which changes the computation. The GGUF files, the MTP head, the films and the SHA-256 of every file are at https://huggingface.co/sirfyyn/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-FLE-GGUF, under the Qwen Community License 1.0 of the base model. The raw measurement data are not part of this release.
